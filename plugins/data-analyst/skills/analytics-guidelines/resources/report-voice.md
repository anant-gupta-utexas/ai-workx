# Report voice

Writing rules for anything a stakeholder reads. Referenced by `present-analysis`, `frame-analysis`, and `metric-design` when they produce a saved artifact.

## Core rules

- **Plain and easy to understand.** Write so a busy non-analyst gets it on one read. If a sentence needs a second read, rewrite it.
- **Actionable and straight to the point.** No fluff, no throat-clearing ("It's worth noting that..."), no hedging filler.
- **Numbers over adjectives.** "Grew a lot" is weak. "Grew 42% in three months" is strong. Lead with the number and, wherever the analysis supports it, a comparison — a baseline, a prior period, a benchmark.
- **Short bullets, one idea each.** If a bullet needs a second sentence to land, it's usually two bullets.
- **Bold the part that matters.** Especially in a takeaways section. Don't over-bold — if everything is bold, nothing stands out.
- **No em dashes, ever.** Use a period, a comma, or a colon instead.
- **No emojis.**
- **Drop analyst jargon when writing to a stakeholder.** Words like "pressure-test," "grain," "reconcile," "plateau," "model assumption," "outlook" don't belong in a recommendation aimed at a business audience. If you wouldn't say it out loud to a busy executive, rewrite it.

## Chart and finding headings

- A chart's heading is its single biggest takeaway, written as one full sentence that reads like a headline: "Mobile users churn twice as fast as desktop users." Not a list of everything the chart shows.
- Observations under a chart are two or three short bullets with the extra things the chart shows, written to help the reader interpret it — they don't repeat the heading.

## Recommendations vs. internal to-dos

- A recommendation is something the audience can act on, decide, plan around, or fund. If it's really an internal data or engineering task (rebuild a pipeline, fix a column, add a data source), it doesn't belong in a stakeholder-facing recommendations section — put it in an internal caveats note instead.
- Tie each recommendation to the number or consequence that makes it matter, so the reader knows why it's there.
